Colombia, 1,900 metres above the sea

It starts on a mountain.

Before it was coffee, it was a cherry on a hillside, picked by hand by someone we know by name. This is how it finds its way to your cup.

Begin the story

Granja Paraiso 92, Cauca, Colombia

1,900 m

Grown by someone with a name

Wilton Benitez farms the slopes above Piendamó. He picks only the ripest cherries, sorts them by size and density, and washes them in ozonated water and ultraviolet light before anything else happens.

Most coffee is sold as "100% Arabica" from somewhere. Ours arrives with a farm, a family and an altitude.

The fermentation tanks

116 hrs

Patience, then a shock

The cherries ferment without oxygen, in a double ferment that runs 116 hours. Then they are rinsed hot, then cold. The thermal shock locks the flavour in place.

It is slow and risky, and few farms attempt it. It is also the reason a coffee can taste like something you would never expect.

And this is what it tastes like.

Nothing is added after roasting. Every note comes from the farm, the fermentation and the roast.

    Roasted so you can taste the farm

    Rare coffee is easy to ruin. Take it too dark and the fruit turns to bitterness, and you are left with the same cup as everywhere else.

    So we roast in small batches, in our own shop, each lot to show what makes it different. Then we taste it before it goes on the shelf.

    "We source intentionally, roast deliberately and quality-control relentlessly. Not out of habit, but out of care."
    Joseph, founder and roaster

    Then it is yours

    It arrives whole bean, so it is still at its best when you grind it. Pour slowly and the room fills with it.

    A bag makes about fifteen cups, a little over two dollars each. A mountain in Colombia, a farmer with a name, and a roaster down the street, every morning.
